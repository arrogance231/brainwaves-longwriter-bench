The vending machine clicked again, that same tired sound it had made every time anyone pressed a button all afternoon. Arro watched the coin slot glow faintly, then turned away before he could press another one. He didn’t need another drink. He needed to stop pretending this conversation was about anything other than what it was.

Minami leaned against the wall beside the machine, arms crossed, looking at him with an expression that wasn’t quite impatient but wasn’t patient either. “You’re doing it again,” he said.

“Doing what?”

“The thing where you listen like you care but your eyes are somewhere else.”

Arro opened his mouth, closed it. The hallway outside was empty now—afternoon classes had let out an hour ago, and most of the third-years were already packing up for the weekend. The fluorescent lights hummed overhead, casting everything in that flat, institutional white that made everyone look slightly washed out. Even Minami, who usually looked sharp even in sweatpants and a hoodie, looked tired under it.

“I’m listening,” Arro said finally.

“You’re planning.” Minami pushed off the wall and stepped closer. “You’ve been planning since we walked in here. I can see it in your face. You’re thinking about how to fix something that doesn’t need fixing.”

“It’s not—”

“It’s always something, isn’t it? You see a problem and you have to solve it. You see someone unhappy and you have to make them happy. You see me single and you have to set me up with someone.” Minami’s voice was quiet, but there was an edge to it. “When did you become so good at knowing what people need before they do?”

Arro felt heat rise in his neck. He hadn’t meant for it to sound like that. He’d just been trying to help. Minami had been lonely since their second year, when Yuki moved to Tokyo for her family business and didn’t come back. Everyone knew Minami liked girls, but no one knew if he wanted to date right now, or if he was waiting for the right person, or if he’d just given up entirely. Arro had assumed the last option. He’d assumed wrong.

“I didn’t mean to—”

“You don’t get to mean things differently than they land, Arro.” Minami’s jaw tightened. “I know you’re worried about me. I know you think you’re being a good friend. But you’re not asking me what I want. You’re deciding for me. And that’s not friendship. That’s control.”

The word hung in the air between them, heavy and uncomfortable. Arro wanted to argue, to say that Minami was overreacting, that he’d only suggested the café because it was low-pressure and public and safe. But Minami was right. He hadn’t asked. He’d decided. Just like he’d decided that Akari needed someone to talk to, that Shiori needed someone to notice her, that everyone around him needed something he thought they should have.

He’d never stopped to think that maybe he was the one who needed something.

“I’m sorry,” Arro said, and meant it.

Minami studied him for a long moment, then nodded once, sharply. “Don’t be sorry. Just… ask next time. Before you plan. Before you decide. Ask.”

“I will.”

“And Arro?” Minami paused at the door, hand on the frame. “Stop trying to fix me. I’m not broken.”

Then he was gone, and Arro was alone in the hallway with the humming lights and the vending machine and the weight of everything he’d been avoiding.

He sat down on the floor, back against the wall, and pulled his knees to his chest. His phone buzzed in his pocket—probably Akari, checking in, probably Shiori, wondering why he’d disappeared. He didn’t answer either. He couldn’t face them yet. Not until he figured out what he was going to say.

What he was going to feel.

Because the truth was, he was terrified. Not of Minami’s anger—not really—but of what would happen if he stopped running. If he stopped planning and fixing and helping everyone else and actually looked at himself. At what he wanted. At who he wanted.

At Akari.

The name surfaced unbidden, and Arro closed his eyes tight. He’d known for weeks. Maybe longer. It had started small—a lingering glance across the classroom, a brush of hands when passing a textbook, the way her laugh sounded different from anyone else’s. He’d told himself it was nothing. That it was just gratitude, or admiration, or the natural closeness of two people living together in a shared program. But it wasn’t. It never had been.

And now Minami knew. Or suspected. Or at least saw through the carefully constructed wall Arro had built around his own feelings. And Shiori—Shiori definitely knew. She’d been watching him for months, probably years, seeing everything he tried to hide. She’d never said anything, never called him out, but her silence was louder than any accusation.

He pulled his phone out again. Three missed calls from Akari. One text: *Where are you? We’re supposed to study.*

He stared at the screen, heart pounding. He could lie. Say he was sick. Say he’d lost track of time. Say anything to avoid going back to the room, to facing her, to admitting that every time she smiled at him, something inside him cracked a little more.

But Minami had said it. *Ask next time.*

So Arro typed: *I’m coming back. Give me ten minutes.*

He hit send, stood up, and walked toward the dormitory.

The shared room was quiet when he got there. Akari was sitting at the desk, textbooks spread out in front of her, pen moving steadily across a notebook. She looked up when he entered, and for a second, Arro thought she might be angry. Her eyebrows were furrowed, her lips pressed into a thin line. But then her expression softened, and she set the pen down.

“You’re late,” she said, but there was no real bite to it.

“I know.” Arro dropped his bag by the door and sat down on the edge of his bed. “Minami and I… talked.”

Akari’s eyes narrowed slightly. “Is he okay?”

“He’ll be fine.” Arro rubbed his temples. “We had a disagreement. About… me trying to help too much. Without asking.”

Akari tilted her head. “That sounds like Arro.”

A small smile tugged at the corner of his mouth. “Thanks.”

“Yeah,” Arro said quickly. “Just… catching up.”

Shiori didn’t look convinced, but she didn’t push. Instead, she sat down on the couch and sipped her coffee, watching them with an expression that was equal parts amusement and concern.

And maybe, that was enough.
